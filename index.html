About

My training was in physics, and I still think like a physicist. When I look at the ocean I want to understand why it does what it does, and I keep going until I can write the reason down, at least roughly.

The ocean is a good place for this kind of question. It has motions on all scales, from the basin-scale circulation down to turbulence of a few centimetres, and the small scales matter: they control how heat and salt are mixed, and through that what the large-scale circulation can do. What the ocean does for the climate, moving heat and carbon around the planet and melting ice at the edges, depends on processes in its interior and at its boundaries, where we have the fewest measurements and where climate models have to rely on parameterisations.

My questions usually come from observations. There is something interesting in the data, I try to find the mechanism behind it, and I write down a model that is simple enough to understand. I test the model with idealised simulations and go back to the observations to see whether they agree; when the question needs it I also do realistic modelling. A theory is only useful if it can be compared with what is actually measured, so I value observations a great deal, even though my own work is theory and modelling. This mix of applied mathematics, physics and earth science is geophysical fluid dynamics, and I am always happy to talk with people who work on fluids with similar tools.

Research

What I work on

Why is the Arctic interior arranged in steps, and how much heat do they let through?

Below the Arctic sea ice there is enough heat to melt all of it. A strong salinity stratification, with staircases in it, keeps the heat down.

Warm Atlantic water sits a few hundred metres below the ice. It is held there by a strong salinity stratification, and within that stratification the temperature and salinity profiles are arranged in staircases: well-mixed layers separated by sharp interfaces. Each interface limits the upward heat flux, and the turbulence inside the layers is double-diffusive. Whether these staircases survive as the Arctic warms and freshens is part of what determines how fast the ice feels the heat below it.

In my PhD I proposed a mechanism for how the staircases form, an instability that appears when double diffusion and stratified turbulence act together, and tested it with direct numerical simulations. I then looked at how much heat passes through a staircase when turbulence is added. What I would like to know now is what happens to them as the Arctic changes.

How does the ocean mix in its bottom boundary layer?

The deep ocean has to come back up along its sloping boundaries. The mixing that lifts it happens in a thin layer at the bottom, and we have only recently started to measure it.

Dense water sinks at the poles and fills the abyss. For the circulation to close it has to rise again, and the current picture is that it does so in the turbulent boundary layer along the sloping sea floor, where the tide works against rough topography. Canyons are where this is most intense. In 2021 the BLT (Boundary Layer Turbulence) experiment, a US–UK field programme funded by NSF and NERC, released dye near the floor of a canyon in the Rockall Trough, west of Ireland, and the dye rose at about a hundred metres per day.

The moorings in the canyon recorded a tidal current with a vertical structure that a propagating internal wave cannot explain. I found that the canyon acts as a resonator: the tide excites a standing internal wave between the canyon walls, and a model with only this mechanism reproduces the observations. The next steps are to extend these dynamics from the canyon to the sloping boundary more generally, and to quantify what canyon mixing does to the global circulation.

How does ocean turbulence carry heat to the ice?

Warm fjord water melts the ice. How fast depends on a turbulent boundary layer a few centimetres thick.

In summer 2026 the GIANT cruise on the RRS Sir David Attenborough went to the fjords of East Greenland to measure melting at a glacier face directly, with uncrewed vehicles surveying along the ice, moorings and profilers in the fjord, and instruments on the glacier. Subglacial discharge leaves the base of the glacier and rises along the ice face as a plume; the plume entrains warm fjord water and drives the melting. The photograph shows one of these plumes reaching the surface.

My part is the boundary layer at the ice face. I use large-eddy simulations that resolve the turbulence next to the ice to understand how this thin layer is maintained, how it transports heat to the ice as the flow changes from buoyancy-driven to shear-driven, and how to turn this into a melt parameterisation for ocean and climate models.

How does double-diffusive convection work, and what is its effect on the large-scale ocean?

Heat diffuses about a hundred times faster than salt. This makes a kind of turbulence that only exists when both of them set the density.

Seawater density depends on both temperature and salinity, and because the two diffuse at very different rates, a water column that is stable in density can still convect: salt fingers when warm salty water lies over cold fresh water, diffusive convection when it is the other way round. This is a special class of turbulence. It is driven by potential energy rather than shear, it has its own instabilities and length scales, and it tends to build large structures, layers, interfaces and staircases, out of small ones. Much of the mid- and low-latitude ocean is in one of these states. I want to understand how it works, from the instability to the mixing it produces, and then to say when it matters compared with everything else.

I have worked out the energetics of salt fingers, separating stirring from irreversible mixing and turning that into a parameterisation of the fluxes, and a theory for how salt-finger staircases form and merge, which matches twenty years of observations in the Mediterranean. What I am working on now is how horizontal stirring, with no unstable gradient in the vertical at all, can generate double-diffusive convection and transport buoyancy up the gradient.

How does energy get from the large scales of the ocean down to the scale of mixing?

Energy enters the ocean at the scale of basins, eddies and tides, and the mixing happens at centimetres. What happens in between?

The ocean has many dynamical regimes: geostrophic eddies, submesoscale fronts, internal waves, stratified turbulence, three-dimensional turbulence. Each of them is reasonably well understood on its own. What we understand much less is how they are connected. Energy enters at large scales and is dissipated at small scales, but the route is not a simple cascade. Geostrophic turbulence moves energy to larger scales, not smaller, and the transfer into waves and turbulence happens at particular places through particular mechanisms. How energy is handed from one regime to the next, and how much goes each way, is the open part of the problem.

I have worked on this from two ends: with the Michigan team, high-resolution regional models that follow the internal-wave cascade down to its dissipation; and from microstructure data, where turbulent dissipation turns out to follow a lognormal mixture, a statistical fingerprint of more than one pathway at work. What I most want to understand next is the last link in the chain: how strongly stratified turbulence (the layered, anisotropic "LAST" regime) is generated from the waves and currents above it, and how it does the mixing.

How are internal waves generated, how do they propagate, and how do they break?

Internal waves are the waves on the density stratification of the ocean. Most of the mixing in the interior happens where they break.

Internal waves come in many kinds: internal tides, hundreds of kilometres long, generated where the tide flows over ridges and canyons; near-inertial waves generated by storms at the surface; lee waves over rough topography in a deep current; and a continuum of smaller waves that exchange energy among themselves. Most of the turbulence in the ocean interior comes from these waves breaking, so they are at the centre of the mixing problem.

Their dynamics are complicated, and this is what makes them interesting fluid problems: how a wave is generated, how it propagates and scatters, how it passes its energy to other waves and in the end to turbulence. These questions come up in most of what I do, and I am interested in them for their own sake.
